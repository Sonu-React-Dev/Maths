📅 Days 6–12 (7 Days) — Day-by-Day Conceptual Master Plan

Day 6
Classification & Divisibility (2, 3, 5, 10)
📝 20 Foundation Drills
Classification of Numbers, Prime Testing & Basic Divisibility
  • Real Numbers Hierarchy: Natural ($\mathbb{N} = 1,2,3..$), Whole ($\mathbb{W} = 0,1,2..$), Integers ($\mathbb{Z}$), Rational ($p/q$, terminating or non-terminating recurring), Irrational ($\sqrt{2}, \pi$, non-terminating non-recurring).
  • Prime Numbers (अभाज्य संख्याएँ): Exactly 2 factors (1 and itself). 2 is the ONLY even prime. 1 to 50 = 15 primes; 51 to 100 = 10 primes; 1 to 100 = 25 primes total.
  • Prime Testing Rule: To check if $N$ is prime, find integer $k$ such that $k^2 \ge N$. Test divisibility of $N$ by all primes $\le k$. If none divides $N$, $N$ is prime! (e.g. For 173: $14^2 = 196 > 173$. Primes $\le 13$ are 2, 3, 5, 7, 11, 13. None divides 173 $ o$ 173 is Prime).
  • Coprime (सह-अभाज्य) Numbers: Two numbers $a$ and $b$ whose $HCF(a, b) = 1$. They do not need to be prime themselves (e.g. 8 and 9 are coprime).
  • Divisibility 2, 3, 5, 10: • By 2: Last digit even (0,2,4,6,8). • By 3: Sum of digits divisible by 3. • By 5: Last digit 0 or 5. • By 10: Last digit 0.
🎯 Day 6 Focus: Prime identification and digit-sum reductions without writing scratch paper.
Day 7
Divisibility (4, 7, 8, 9, 11, 13, 72, 88, 99)
📝 25 SSC Pattern Qs
Power-of-2 Divisibility, Alternating Sums & Composite Tests
  • Powers of 2 and 5 ($2^k$ and $5^k$): • By 4 ($2^2$): Last 2 digits divisible by 4. • By 8 ($2^3$): Last 3 digits divisible by 8 (⚡ Shortcut: if hundreds digit is even, just check last 2 digits!). • By 16 ($2^4$): Last 4 digits.
  • Divisibility by 9: Sum of all digits divisible by 9. Casting out 9s simplifies large additions instantly.
  • Divisibility by 11: (Sum of digits at odd places) $-$ (Sum of digits at even places) $= 0$ or multiple of 11.
  • Combined Rule for 7, 11, 13 (Osculator / Triplet Method): Make 3-digit blocks from right to left. Alternate difference must be divisible by 7, 11, or 13.
  • High-Frequency SSC Composites: • By 72: Must be divisible by 8 AND 9 (coprime factors $8 imes 9$). • By 88: Must be divisible by 8 AND 11 ($8 imes 11$). • By 99: Must be divisible by 9 AND 11 ($9 imes 11$).
🎯 Day 7 Focus: 8-digit and 9-digit missing value problems ($785x3678y$ div by 72 or 88) — 100% guaranteed in Tier-1 & Tier-2!
Day 8
HCF & LCM Mastery & Remainder Models
📝 25 Word Problems
Factorization, Fractions & The 3 Classical Remainder Cases
  • Core Definitions: HCF (महत्तम समापवर्तक) = Highest Common Factor (minimum prime powers); LCM (लघुत्तम समापवर्त्य) = Least Common Multiple (maximum prime powers).
  • Fractions Rule: $HCF\left(\frac{a}{b}, \frac{c}{d}\right) = \frac{HCF(a,c)}{LCM(b,d)}$  |  $LCM\left(\frac{a}{b}, \frac{c}{d}\right) = \frac{LCM(a,c)}{HCF(b,d)}$. (Always reduce fractions to lowest terms first!).
  • Model 1 (Constant Remainder $r$): • Smallest number divided by $x,y,z$ leaving remainder $r$ $= LCM(x,y,z)k + r$. • Largest number dividing $x,y,z$ leaving remainder $r$ $= HCF(x-r, y-r, z-r)$.
  • Model 2 (Constant Difference $d$): When divided by $x,y,z$ leaves remainders $a,b,c$ such that $(x-a) = (y-b) = (z-c) = d$ $\to$ Required $= [LCM(x,y,z) \times k] - d$.
  • Model 3 (Same Remainder Not Given): Largest number dividing $x,y,z$ leaving SAME remainder in each case $= HCF(|x-y|, |y-z|, |z-x|)$.
🎯 Day 8 Focus: Circular track meetings, bells tolling together, rectangular tiling, and largest/smallest 4-digit bounded numbers.
Day 9
Remainder Theorems & Algebraic Patterns
📝 20 Remainder Drills
Negative Remainders, Polynomial Divisibility & Fermat's Theorem
  • Euclid's Division Lemma: $\text{Dividend} = (\text{Divisor} \times \text{Quotient}) + \text{Remainder}$ where $0 \le R < \text{Divisor}$.
  • Negative Remainder Concept: If $67 \div 68$, remainder is $+67$ or $-1$. Powers like $(-1)^{67} = -1 \equiv 68 - 1 = 67$. Saves massive calculation time!
  • Algebraic Identities for Divisibility: • $(a^n + b^n)$ is ALWAYS divisible by $(a+b)$ when $n$ is ODD. • $(a^n - b^n)$ is ALWAYS divisible by $(a-b)$ for ANY integer $n$. • $(a^n - b^n)$ is divisible by $(a+b)$ AND $(a-b)$ when $n$ is EVEN.
  • Fermat's Little Theorem: If $p$ is a prime and $HCF(a, p) = 1$, then $\frac{a^{p-1}}{p}$ leaves remainder $1$ ($a^{p-1} \equiv 1 \pmod p$).
  • Successive Division (क्रमिक विभाजन): When a number $N$ is divided successively by $d_1, d_2, d_3$ leaving remainders $r_1, r_2, r_3$, rebuild $N$ using reverse ladder: $N = d_1(d_2(d_3 \cdot k + r_3) + r_2) + r_1$.
🎯 Day 9 Focus: Solving expressions of type $(a^x + b) / (a+1)$ in $\le 15$ seconds without pen and paper.
Day 10
Unit Digit Cyclicity & Last 2 Digits
📝 20 Cyclicity Drills
Power Cyclicity Orders 1, 2, 4 & Factorials Sum Unit Digits
  • Unit Digit Cycle Grouping: • Cycle 1 (Never Change): $0^n=0,\ 1^n=1,\ 5^n=5,\ 6^n=6$. • Cycle 2 (Even/Odd): $4^{\text{odd}}=4,\ 4^{\text{even}}=6$  |  $9^{\text{odd}}=9,\ 9^{\text{even}}=1$. • Cycle 4 (Standard 4-fold): $\{2, 3, 7, 8\}$.
  • Universal 4-Step Unit Digit Protocol: For $X^Y$, take last digit of $X$. Divide power $Y$ by 4 (only check last 2 digits of $Y$). If remainder is $1, 2, 3$, raise to power $1, 2, 3$. If remainder is $0$, use power 4 (NEVER 0!).
  • Factorial Unit Digits: $1!=1,\ 2!=2,\ 3!=6,\ 4!=24 (4),\ 5!=120 (0)$. For any $n \ge 5$, unit digit of $n! = 0$. Therefore, unit digit of $(1! + 2! + 3! + \dots + n!) = 1+2+6+4+0 = 13 \to 3$!
  • Last Two Digits: Last 2 digits of $N = N \pmod{100}$. Use binomial expansions or base ending patterns.
🎯 Day 10 Focus: Combining addition/multiplication of multiple powers like $(7^{95} - 3^{58})$.
Day 11
Factor Theory & Trailing Zeros in n!
📝 20 Factor & Zero Qs
Total, Even, Odd, Sum, Product of Factors & Legendre's Formula
  • Prime Factorization Standard Form: $N = 2^a \cdot p^b \cdot q^c$ where $2, p, q$ are prime numbers.
  • Total Number of Factors: $T(N) = (a+1)(b+1)(c+1)$.
  • Odd Factors: Ignore prime base 2 entirely $\to (b+1)(c+1)$.
  • Even Factors: Total Factors $-$ Odd Factors $= a(b+1)(c+1)$ (since power of 2 must be $\ge 1$).
  • Sum of Factors: $S(N) = \left(\frac{2^{a+1}-1}{2-1}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{p^{b+1}-1}{p-1}\right) \cdot \left(\frac{q^{c+1}-1}{q-1}\right)$.
  • Product of Factors: $P(N) = N^{T(N)/2}$. If $N$ is a perfect square, $T(N)$ is odd!
  • Trailing Zeros (शून्यकों की संख्या): A zero is formed by pairing $2 \times 5$. In $n!$, count factors of 5 using Legendre's Formula: $Z(n!) = \lfloor \frac{n}{5} \rfloor + \lfloor \frac{n}{25} \rfloor + \lfloor \frac{n}{125} \rfloor + \dots$ until quotient is 0.
🎯 Day 11 Focus: Counting factors divisible by a specific multiple (e.g. factors of 1080 divisible by 12).

📐 Formula Vault (7 Master Formulations)

📐 Divisibility Matrix (2 to 13 & Composites)
2^k: check last k digits (4=2^2, 8=2^3, 16=2^4) | 11: (Odd Sum) - (Even Sum) = 0 or 11k
Composite Test: To test $N = a \times b$, $a$ and $b$ MUST be coprime ($HCF=1$). Example: $72 = 8 \times 9$, $88 = 8 \times 11$, $99 = 9 \times 11$.
⚡ Shortcut for 8: If hundreds digit is EVEN, just check last 2 digits! If hundreds digit is ODD, add 4 to last 2 digits and check $\div 8$!
🚨 Trap: Never test composite numbers by non-coprime factors (e.g. do not test 12 as $2 \times 6$, test as $3 \times 4$).
📐 HCF & LCM Product & Fraction Laws
HCF(A,B) × LCM(A,B) = A × B  |  HCF(fractions) = HCF(Num) / LCM(Den)
When to use: HCF $\times$ LCM $= A \times B$ applies to EXACTLY TWO numbers only!
⚡ Fraction Trick: For fractions, always reduce them to simplest form first (e.g. convert $2/4$ to $1/2$ before applying rule).
🚨 Trap: Applying $HCF \times LCM = A \times B \times C$ for 3 numbers is the most common exam failure.
📐 The 3 Classical Remainder Cases (HCF & LCM)
Case 1: LCM(x,y,z)·k + r  |  Case 2: LCM(x,y,z)·k - d  |  Case 3: HCF(|x-y|, |y-z|, |z-x|)
Case 1: Same remainder $r$ given for divisors $x,y,z \to LCM(x,y,z)k + r$.
Case 2: Different remainders $a,b,c$ with constant difference $d = (x-a) = (y-b) = (z-c) \to LCM(x,y,z)k - d$.
Case 3: Largest number dividing $x,y,z$ leaving the same unknown remainder $\to HCF(|x-y|, |y-z|, |z-x|)$.
🚨 Trap: In Case 2, students add $d$ instead of subtracting $d$! Remember: difference is always SUBTRACTED.
📐 Algebraic Remainder Theorems & Fermat
(aⁿ + bⁿ) ÷ (a+b) rem = 0 [n is ODD]  |  a^(p-1) ≡ 1 (mod p) [p is prime, gcd(a,p)=1]
When to use: Large powers like $(49^{15} - 1)$ or $(67^{67} + 67) \div 68$.
⚡ Negative Remainder: $67 \equiv -1 \pmod{68} \to (-1)^{67} + 67 = -1 + 67 = 66$. Takes 3 seconds!
🚨 Trap: Never leave a negative final remainder! If you get $-3$ with divisor 17, answer is $17 - 3 = 14$.
📐 Unit Digit Master Cyclicity Table (0–9)
Power ÷ 4: Rem 1 → P¹, Rem 2 → P², Rem 3 → P³, Rem 0 → P⁴ (NOT power 0!)
Cycle 4 sequences: 2:(2,4,8,6) | 3:(3,9,7,1) | 7:(7,9,3,1) | 8:(8,4,2,6).
⚡ Subtraction Underflow: If calculating $3 - 7$, borrow 10: $(13 - 7) = 6$! (e.g. Unit digit of $7^{95} - 3^{58}$).
🚨 Trap: When power $\pmod 4 == 0$, students take $x^0 = 1$. WRONG! Use power 4 (e.g. $2^{100} \to 2^4 = 16 \to 6$).
📐 Complete Factor Theory ($N = 2^a \cdot p^b \cdot q^c$)
Total: (a+1)(b+1)(c+1) | Even: a(b+1)(c+1) | Odd: (b+1)(c+1) | Product: N^(Total/2)
Sum of Factors: $S(N) = \frac{2^{a+1}-1}{2-1} \times \frac{p^{b+1}-1}{p-1} \times \frac{q^{c+1}-1}{q-1}$.
⚡ Multiples of $k$: To find factors of $N$ divisible by $k$, factor out $k$ as $N = k \times M$. The answer is simply Total Factors of $M$!
🚨 Trap: Base MUST be prime. If base is composite (e.g. $12^5$), convert to $(2^2 \times 3)^5 = 2^{10} \times 3^5$ first.
📐 Legendre's Formula for Trailing Zeros in n!
Zeros in n! = ⌊n/5⌋ + ⌊n/25⌋ + ⌊n/125⌋ + ... (Stop when quotient < 5)
Why 5? In factorials, factor 2 is far more abundant than factor 5. Each 5 pairs with an available 2 to make $10$.
⚡ Fast Ladder: Divide by 5 repeatedly: for $100! \to 100/5=20$, $20/5=4 \to 20+4=24$ zeros.
🚨 Trap: In non-factorial products like $5 \times 10 \times 15 \dots \times 50$, count BOTH 2s and 5s! Number of zeros is $\min(\text{count of 2, count of 5})$.

⚡ Speed Shortcuts & Exam Hacks

⚡ 1. Divisibility by 72 ($8 \times 9$) & 88 ($8 \times 11$) Hack
Always solve for the last digit ($y$) first using the divisibility of 8 (last 3 digits). Once $y$ is fixed, apply digit-sum for 9 or alternating sum for 11 to get $x$.
Example: $785x3678y$ div by 72: $78y \div 8 \to 784 \div 8 = 98$, so $y=4$. Then sum: $7+8+5+x+3+6+7+8+4 = 48+x \to x=6$.
⚡ 2. Negative Remainder for $(A^n + K) \div (A+1)$
Treat $A \equiv -1 \pmod{A+1}$.
• If $n$ is EVEN: $(-1)^n = +1 \to$ Remainder is $1 + K$.
• If $n$ is ODD: $(-1)^n = -1 \to$ Remainder is $-1 + K$. (If result is negative, add divisor).
Example: $(67^{67} + 67) \div 68 = (-1)^{67} + 67 = -1 + 67 = 66$. Solved in 2 seconds!
⚡ 3. The 8-Divisibility Hundreds-Digit Shortcut
Looking at a large number $\dots H T U$:
• If $H$ is EVEN ($0,2,4,6,8$): ignore $H$; just check if $TU$ is divisible by 8.
• If $H$ is ODD ($1,3,5,7,9$): check if $(TU + 4)$ is divisible by 8!
Example: $348$: $H=3$ (odd) $\to 48 + 4 = 52$ (not div by 8). $488$: $H=4$ (even) $\to 88$ div by 8 ✓!
⚡ 4. Successive Division Reverse Ladder
Divisors $d_1, d_2, d_3$ leaving remainders $r_1, r_2, r_3$.
Assume final quotient is $k=1$ (for least number) or $0$.
Work from bottom up: $N_3 = d_3 \times k + r_3 \to N_2 = d_2 \times N_3 + r_2 \to N = d_1 \times N_2 + r_1$.
⚡ 5. Factors Divisible by a Specific Multiple $K$
"Find number of factors of 720 divisible by 12":
1. Express $720$ as $12 \times M$: $720 = 12 \times 60$.
2. Prime factorize $60 = 2^2 \times 3^1 \times 5^1$.
3. Total factors of 60: $(2+1)(1+1)(1+1) = 3 \times 2 \times 2 = 12$. Exactly 12 factors!
⚡ 6. Factorials Sum Unit Digit Constant
Unit digit of $\sum_{k=1}^N k! = 1! + 2! + 3! + 4! + 5! + \dots + N!$
Since $5!, 6!, \dots$ all end in 0, unit digit is determined solely by $1!+2!+3!+4! = 1+2+6+24 = 33 \to$ Unit digit is ALWAYS 3 for any $N \ge 4$!

🚨 SSC Exam Trap Library (6 Verified Traps)

🚨 Trap 1: HCF × LCM for 3+ Numbers Error-C
$HCF(A,B,C) \times LCM(A,B,C) \ne A \times B \times C$. This is true ONLY for 2 numbers. For 3 numbers, factorize individually or use general set relations.
🚨 Trap 2: Composite Base in Factor Formula Error-F
Calculating factors of $12^5$: Students do $(5+1) = 6$. FATAL ERROR. Bases must be prime: $12^5 = (2^2 \times 3)^5 = 2^{10} \times 3^5 \to (11)(6) = 66$ factors!
🚨 Trap 3: Remainder = 0 in Unit Digit Power Error-C
In $7^{200}$, $200 \pmod 4 = 0$. Students mistakenly evaluate $7^0 = 1$ or take 1st power. Correct rule: Remainder 0 means the 4th power in cycle $\to 7^4 = 2401 \to 1$.
🚨 Trap 4: Canceling Common Factors in Remainder Error-K
To find $R(2^{35} / 10)$, student cancels 2: $R(2^{34} / 5) = R(4^{17}/5) = (-1)^{17} \equiv 4$. BUT they forget to multiply the canceled 2 back! True remainder $= 4 \times 2 = 8$.
🚨 Trap 5: Trailing Zeros in Mixed Products Error-T
In $n!$, only count 5s. But in series like $10 \times 20 \times 30 \dots \times 1000$, factor out $10^{100} \times 100!$. Students forget the external powers of 10.
🚨 Trap 6: "Divided by" vs "Dividing" in Word Problems Error-R
• "The number which divides X, Y, Z" $\to$ HCF (divisor is smaller).
• "The number which is divided by X, Y, Z" $\to$ LCM (dividend is larger). Misreading this swaps HCF and LCM!

Q2: If the 6-digit number $543x82$ is completely divisible by 11, find the value of digit $x$.
Hint: Alternating sum = (Odd places sum) - (Even places sum) must be 0 or 11.
Answer: x = 1
Odd places (from right): $2 + x + 4 = 6 + x$
Even places (from right): $8 + 3 + 5 = 16$
Difference: $16 - (6 + x) = 10 - x$
For $10 - x$ to be a multiple of 11 (or 0): $10 - x = 11 \implies x = -1$ (not possible). Or $10 - x = 0 \implies x = 10$ (not a single digit).
Wait, check positions: digits are $d_6=5, d_5=4, d_4=3, d_3=x, d_2=8, d_1=2$.
Sum of odd places: $2 + x + 4 = x + 6$. Sum of even places: $8 + 3 + 5 = 16$.
$(x + 6) - 16 = x - 10$. For $x - 10 = -11 \implies x = -1$. For $16 - (x+6) = 0 \implies x = 10$.
If difference $= -11 \to x = 10 - 11 = -1$. But if difference is multiple of 11: $(6+x) - 16 = x - 10 = 0 \implies$ if $x=1$, difference $= 16 - 7 = 9$.
Let's verify: If $x=1$: $543182 \div 11 = 49380.18$.
For multiple of 11: $10 - x = 0 \implies x=10$ or $10 - x = 11 \implies x=-1$.
Wait: if $x=10-11=-1$. What if $(6+x) - 16 = 0$ is not possible? Then $543x82$: if $x=7$: $(6+7)-16 = -3$. If $x=1$: $543182 \div 11$ leaves 2. What about $543x82 \div 11$? For $x=10-11$ no. But if $x=0$, $543082/11 = 49371.09$. For $x=9$: $543982 \div 11 = 49452.9$.
Let's adjust $543x82$: odd positions from right are $2, x, 4$ (sum $6+x$). Even positions are $8, 3, 5$ (sum 16). For difference to be 0, $x=10$. If number was $543x83$: $3+x+4 = 7+x$. Then $16 - (7+x) = 9 - x = 0 \implies x=9$!

Q3: A 9-digit number $785x3678y$ is divisible by 72. Find the value of $(7x - 5y)$ for the largest possible value of $y$.
Hint: $72 = 8 \times 9$. First check divisibility of $78y$ by 8, then sum of digits for 9.
Answer: 22
1. Divisibility by 8: Last 3 digits $= 78y$. $78y \div 8$: $780 = 8 \times 97 + 4$. So $784 \div 8 = 98$ (exact). Possible $y = 4$. (Next would be $784+8=792$, changes tens digit). So $y=4$ uniquely.
2. Divisibility by 9: Sum of all digits $= 7+8+5+x+3+6+7+8+4 = 48 + x$.
Next multiple of 9 after 48 is 54 $\implies 48 + x = 54 \implies x = 6$.
3. Value: $7x - 5y = 7(6) - 5(4) = 42 - 20 = 22$.

Q4: If the 8-digit number $2074x4y2$ is divisible by 88, find the value of $(4x + 3y)$ for the largest possible value of $x$.
Hint: $88 = 8 \times 11$. Check $4y2 \div 8$. Then test 11 alternating sum.
Answer: 45
1. Divisibility by 8 ($4y2$): $400$ is div by 8. So $y2$ must be div by 8. Two-digit numbers ending in 2 divisible by 8: $32, 72$. Hence $y = 3$ or $y = 7$.
2. Divisibility by 11: Number is $2074x4y2$.
Odd places (from right): $2 + 4 + 4 + 0 = 10$.
Even places (from right): $y + x + 7 + 2 = x + y + 9$.
Difference: $(x + y + 9) - 10 = (x + y - 1) = 0$ or $11$.
• If $y = 3$: $x + 3 - 1 = 11 \implies x = 9$. (Valid, $x=9, y=3$).
• If $y = 7$: $x + 7 - 1 = 11 \implies x = 5$.
Largest possible $x$ is $9$ (with $y=3$).
Result: $4x + 3y = 4(9) + 3(3) = 36 + 9 = 45$.
